There's a kind of watch where the back is clear glass and you can see the movement working.

Dozens of wheels, each one meshed against the next, the whole thing turning inside a space smaller than a coin. The precision in a well-made one is close to absurd. Let two of those wheels drift a hair apart and the watch stops. Everything the wheels behind them were carrying arrives nowhere.

What animates it is a battery.

For us it's creativity. Creative energy moving through a person is the thing that turns the wheels. Everything built downstream of that is architecture for carrying it somewhere.

Most people who like [the flywheel idea](https://www.infinitegameos.io/concepts/creator-flywheel) have the picture of one. A loop, a handful of stages, each feeding the next. It's a beautiful idea and it points in exactly the right direction.

Without the specifics, though, there's no precision.

What changed it for me was defining the laws for one particular flywheel rather than for flywheels in general. A working loop gets smoother with each rotation. Fine. Smoother how, in this loop, at which handoff? Each rotation yields more. More of what, read how?

"Smoother" as a principle is a poster on a wall. Smoother, defined for one specific loop, is a part you can install.

Being thematically right is where all of this starts, because getting the direction right first is exactly the order you want. Precision is the next move rather than a correction of the first one. And once a law is defined tightly enough, it can be measured. Once it can be measured, something other than your memory can watch it.

Here's where it actually slips.

Friction rarely lives inside the stages of your work. It gathers at the handoffs between them. You make something good, a response comes back and then there's nowhere for the response to go. Nothing turns it into a change. The loop stays open.

An open circuit carries no current. The wheels are all sitting right there and two of them stopped touching, so nothing arrives.

The reflex when a handoff snags is to patch it at the receiving end and keep moving. That works. It also guarantees you meet the same snag next rotation. The other move is to ask which structural step is missing at that seam, build the step and let every rotation after that inherit it.

Which brings up the elephant.

AI is very good at specificity, metrics and patterns. That happens to be the exact layer where a beautiful loop becomes a working one.

So the division of labor is cleaner now than it's ever been. You bring the direction, the end state, the trajectory the whole thing points toward and the constraints that feel true in your body. Those are yours. Nothing else can source them. The measurable precision underneath, the watching, the noticing that this one seam has snagged twice this month, can be held by something built to hold it.

There's a real satisfaction in that.

The architect / engineer in you get to build something beautifully precise, pointed at something you actually chose. It happens to be efficient. The results show up as a by-product of the trajectory rather than as the thing you were chasing.

Then you get to step back out of the engineer and go be a person again.

Once the loops carry their own momentum, holding them costs a fraction of what starting them cost. You oversee instead of (micro) manage. The energy comes home.

And then you decide what to do with it. Refine the trajectory. Have more fun. Follow something you're curious about. Start a project. Run a side quest.

If you want a first move on this, it isn't a build step.

Picture the version of your work where the loops are made and turning on their own. You're standing there looking at the thing you built. There's an afternoon in front of you that nobody has claimed.

What do you do with it?

That answer is the reason to build the mechanism. Motivation is easier to find when you already know what's waiting on the other side.

The rest is the work of [building the structure where the energy already flows](https://www.lanebelone.com/blog/f/build-the-mill-where-the-water-flows), then tightening it until the parts touch.

Look at the back of the watch sometime.
